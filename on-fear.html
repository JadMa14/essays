On Fear

There are those who claim that bravery is acting through fear, and others that bravery is the absence of fear. Who is right?

It is tempting to say that those who are acting through fear are the truly brave because it seems like this person is earning their bravery. They feel the emotion telling them to freeze, cower or run away, but they resist it and move head on into danger. This is the hero's image.

But what if our hero was afraid of a butterfly? Would we call him brave who, upon seeing a butterfly entering a room, begins to sweat and tremble? Would we call him brave if he let the butterfly rest on his hand? It seems ludicrous to say yes. If the threshold for fear is so low, how can we expect him to act bravely in the face of true danger?

But people possess irrational fears that do not translate to other dangers. For example, one may be terrified of heights, but find himself capable of taking on great financial risk. One may be terrified of social interaction but may be brave in putting forth a new theory to his peers. In this case, it seems that the cowardliness is localized. The Hound in Game of Thrones is afraid of flames due to the trauma of his youth, but he does not seem to fear much else.

And then there's the man who does not feel fear. This man resembles a child who does not know the true risks of the world yet. This type of bravery can be borne out of sheer stupidity — the person just doesn't assess risk normally. Something would probably be clinically wrong with him. But there's the other kind — the kind who has seen trouble and become habituated with loss. An army veteran, having risked his life a multitude of times, is no longer scared for his life when a gun is pulled at him. This man has familiarized himself with danger such that it doesn't pose much of a threat to him anymore; he is not afraid in the ordinary sense. Thomas Shelby is a good example of a character possessing this kind of bravery. But Thomas Shelby was also probably clinically depressed and suicidal.

So who of these is the true brave man?

Meanwhile, the man without fear at all — whether due to brain damage, ignorance, or desensitization — may look brave but lacks the moral tension that gives courage its substance. A soldier who no longer fears death because he's numb to it is like a man who feels no temptation and thus cannot claim self-control. The absence of fear removes the very obstacle that bravery overcomes.

I think this is the consensus opinion, but I believe it's wrong. The brave man should not be afraid of many things, and he should be able to work through the fear when he feels it is warranted. If a man is afraid of dogs, but not much else, and if he is brave in the face of dogs, then he is a brave man. If he is afraid of darkness, and he cowers in the face of other such trivialities, even if he sometimes avails himself, he is still not brave, for this is not someone you would take to a challenging place with you.

Bravery is thus more than just being able to work through fear, although that is the most admirable part about it; it is also about not being afraid of many trivial things.
